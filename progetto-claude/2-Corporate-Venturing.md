### CV 1-10 — Guest speaker Deutsche Bank (housebank e rischio reputazionale)
_Data: 2026-10-01 · Argomento del syllabus 8: Guest speaker: Deutsche Bank_

Deutsche Bank guest lecture on the bank's global housebank strategy, a worked example of a client's banking journey, and hard-won lessons on reputational risk, followed by Q&A on compliance, cyber, fintech, and hiring.

#### Global Economic Context & DB Strategy
- World shifted from US dominance (close to 40% of GDP) to a multipolar setup with China resurgent; Europe still central to trade corridors.
- Demographics, AI, climate and geopolitical events are reshaping the landscape where DB's strategy begins.
- DB operates in 80-82 countries, pursuing 'glocalization': global reach with strong local adaptation.
    - Starbucks failed in China by exporting a standard model; Pizza Hut succeeded by adapting.
- Italy is DB's second largest European market after Germany, with close to 3,000 employees and a branch network.

#### Global Housebank Concept & Client Journey
- 'Global housebank' means being the client's reference bank everywhere, proactively advising rather than waiting.
- Illustrative journey of a client and his company: from private banking to corporate banking as the firm grows and internationalises.
- Expansion needs pull in cross-border payments, FX hedging, credit facilities vetted by Credit Risk Management, and M&A advisory with fiduciary/trust services.
- Capital markets division builds bespoke securities for sophisticated clients; wealth management handles succession and family continuity.

#### Reputation & Reputational Risk
- Reputation is the collective opinion others hold; fragile like a house of cards, slow to build and quick to destroy.
- DB case: 2011 sale of EUR 8bn Italian BTPs, Frankfurt HQ tax raid, 2015 first loss, IMF/Fed labelling DB the most systemic risk.
    - Stock price collapsed, ratings downgraded, bank came close to default.
- Other cases cited: Theranos/Elizabeth Holmes, Volkswagen emissions, Chiara Ferragni, Burger King's Women's Day post.
- Three recommendations: always tell the truth, stick to the highest ethical standards, treat communication as a serious profession and get expert help.

#### Q&A Highlights
- EU compliance: post-2015 zero-tolerance helped DB recover but now caps returns.
- Cyber: the real 'big one' hasn't hit yet; defence requires cross-industry and public-private alliances; most incidents stem from people, not IT.
- COVID: DB never took government money, relying on its liquidity buffer; bank failures are driven by liquidity, not losses.
- Fintechs vs big tech: Revolut and similar may be crushed once Apple/Google/Amazon enter.

#### Innovation, Growth & Hiring
- Banking product innovation is limited; most spend goes into smarter delivery and internal tools. Insurance has more room to innovate.
- Growth strategy varies by division: hire bankers in wealth management, M&A in asset management, capital allocation in investment banking.
- DB hires for potential (handling complexity in group simulations) and ability to manage change, citing JP Morgan as the benchmark.

### CV 6-10 — Caso SNAM e ambidexterity
_Data: 2026-10-06 · Argomento del syllabus 9: Ambidexterity and Corporate venturing (case: SNAM; Tushman et al.)_

Class discussion of the SNAM case: energy industry structure, SNAM's history and diversification, group strategy recommendations after the Ukraine gas shock, and ambidextrous organization as the key lesson.

#### Energy Industry Fundamentals
- Energy fuels factories (GDP) and households (heating, electricity), so price shocks hit the whole economy.
- Sources: fossil fuels, nuclear, renewables (wind, hydro, solar), hydrogen, biomethane, carbon capture.
- Renewables are weather and context dependent and hard to store; Spain and southern Italy suit them better.
- Fossil infrastructure is centralized and supply-side; emerging sources are decentralized, demand-side, and modular.

#### SNAM History and Diversification
- SNAM grew inside Eni as the gas transport network arm across Italy, Europe, and North Africa.
- Went public around 2001 and separated from Eni around 2012 due to unbundling regulation.
    - Analogy: content, network, and retail separated as in telecom (TIM, Vodafone, Fastweb)
- Diversified via a biomethane subsidiary, Renovit for energy efficiency, hydrogen stakes, and CCS with Eni near Ravenna.
- SNAMTEC open innovation unit built digital and AI tools, including a digital twin of Italy's network.

#### Group Recommendations After the Ukraine Gas Shock
- Prioritize security: keep investing in transport, regasification, and storage, SNAM's historic advantage.
- Consolidate a connected European corridor network.
- Stage hydrogen and other transition bets with milestones; scale only when real demand appears.
- Leverage the cultural shift to flatter structure and keep a separate unit for exploration.

#### Organizational Ambidexterity
- Paradigm shifts destroy competencies and misalign incentives, as with Kodak's salespeople resisting digital.
- Tushman and O'Reilly argue for a dual organization exploiting and exploring at once.
- Two options: 'make' via a separate development unit (SNAMTEC, Plenitude) or 'buy' via corporate venture.
- Examples: IBM's EBO (Emerging Business Opportunities) unit, Procter & Gamble, Amplifon X, Apple buying Beats, Microsoft and OpenAI.

#### Regulation Q&A
- Regulation gives direction but removes price control and adds bureaucracy; neither purely an advantage nor a cost.

### CV 13-10 — Caso Amazon vs Barnes & Noble (dynamic capabilities)
_Data: 2026-10-13 · Argomento del syllabus 11: Building the agile organization (case: Barnes & Noble vs Amazon; Teece; O'Reilly-Tushman)_

Class discussion of the Amazon case: groups analyzed the pre-internet book retail industry, Barnes & Noble's capabilities, Amazon's entry strategy and B&N's failed online response, concluding with sticky capabilities and dynamic capabilities.

#### Book Retail Industry: Good or Bad?
- Market worth 26 billion, expected to reach 33 billion; demand peaks in Q4 and is hard to predict
    - 50,000 new titles yearly, about a million titles in stock
- Publishers: about 40,000, but top 20 hold 88% share; author royalties around 10 to 15%
- Wholesalers (Ingram over 50%, Baker and Taylor) existed to serve small mom-and-pop stores; net margin about 1.5%
    - Returns around 30% of hardcovers, doubling shipping costs
- Verdict: decent, growing industry for dominant players, but low margins, high entry barriers and competition from TV, films, video games

#### Barnes & Noble's Capabilities
- Largest US book retailer, about 2.5 billion sales, roughly 2 to 2.5% net margin, superstore format
- Key capabilities: centralized procurement and warehousing, experiential marketing (cafes, Starbucks, events), IT for logistics
    - Average purchase $20 vs $10 at independent stores
- Real estate finance mattered: long-term leases kept invested capital near 600 million instead of $3 billion

#### Amazon's Entry Strategy
- Bezos targeted the internet (web growth about 2,000% per year); books were a way in, chosen from about 20 categories
- Books fit: no 800-pound gorilla (Borders plus B&N about 14%), huge title count, standardized products
    - 'Sell all, carry few': list 1 million titles, own about 2,000; return rate about 2%
- Software-centric firm: 80% of software spend on back office, managers with computer backgrounds, based in Seattle for tech talent
- Key trick: securing supply from Ingram, a wholesaler in decline seeking new channels

#### Barnes & Noble's Reaction and Takeaways
- barnesandnoble.com was kept separate for tax reasons and underpushed due to cannibalization fears and store manager incentives
- Missed opportunities: in-store pickup, attacking Ingram as Amazon's weak point
- **Lesson: capabilities are sticky; ambidexterity alone is not enough, firms need dynamic capabilities and agility** (Teece et al.)
- Second part of the course, on innovation strategy, starts end of October

### CV 15-10 — Guest speaker ENGIE (transizione energetica, PPA)
_Data: 2026-10-15 · Argomento del syllabus 12: Guest speaker: ENGIE_

Guest lecture by the CEO of ENGIE Italy on geopolitics, Italy's high energy prices, the energy transition, and PPAs as a concrete solution, followed by Q&A.

#### Company Overview
- French CAC 40 company, moved from coal to gas in the 60s and into renewables in the 90s
    - Present in 30 countries; 90,000 people, 8.8 billion EBIT, 57 gigawatt installed renewable capacity
- Energy transition now means three goals: decarbonization, affordability, and security of supply
- Shifting in the last five years from gas networks to power infrastructure; resilient portfolio seen as key to agility
- Exiting Belgian nuclear assets to the Belgian state, since nuclear needs huge investment and carries high operational risk

#### Geopolitics and Italian Energy Prices
- Defocusing from North America because the US government changed rules, removing investment stability
- Italy imports 95% of gas and power is priced by gas, so prices sit well above the European average
- Prices are now structurally "higher for longer", breaking existing business plan assumptions
- European elections pushing energy toward state control and regulation, which the speaker views negatively

#### Strategy: Electrification, Flexibility, Grid
- Core focus for the next five years: electrification, flexibility (BESS storage), and power grids
- In Italy, over 500 megawatts of BESS already authorized; medium BESS of 3 to 5 megawatts now possible on site
- Renewables produced in the south, consumed in the industrial north, so efficient transmission is critical
    - Spain's six-hour blackout cited as a grid management lesson
- Gas remains necessary for now; aim is to shift molecules toward green gas such as biomethane

#### Decarbonization Trends, Barriers and AI
- Fit for 55 targets are lagging in every stream; 2025 was the first year emissions rose instead of fell
- Energy efficiency is underrated; data center waste heat linked to district heating cited as an example
- AI is already here: managers have AI coaches and finance asked for 9% lower costs from 2029 in the plan
- Data center growth limited by grid connections and permitting

#### PPA Example and Q&A
- Heineken case: about 9 MW PV in Sardinia near Ichnusa plant, with on-site and offsite PPAs
    - 45% self-consumed covers the site fully; 55% sent to a northern site at a flat 10-year price
    - Current 40% production-demand match targeted to reach 75% by adding wind and storage
- Similar PPAs exist with Amazon and Google for green power at flat prices
- Speaker expects EU decarbonization targets to be slowed and extended, as happened with automotive

### CV 29-10 — Caso Red Bull (Sustaining Innovation)
_Data: 2026-10-29 · Argomento del syllabus 13: Sustaining Innovation (case: Red Bull; Shane ch.10)_

Class discussion of the Red Bull case: how Red Bull beat Coke through a coherent niche strategy, how Monster did the same to Red Bull, and what Red Bull should do next.

#### Red Bull Origins and the Failed Taste Test
- Company founded 1984 with rights to the Asian product except Thailand; launched in Austria in 1987.
    - 1987 revenues were 1.1 million against 1.4 million spent on advertising
- Product tested badly because it was tested alone, while real use was as a mixer with spirits in clubs.
    - Lesson: replicate the real usage situation, or the test is wrong
- 98% of new consumer goods launches fail; heavy testing often serves management to cover themselves with shareholders.
    - Tests work for quantifiable products (detergent, paint), poorly for taste or style
- Broke even in '99; founder's key skill was salesmanship and building trust to keep raising money.

#### Red Bull's Coherent Niche Strategy vs Coke
- Soft drink market was 170 billion in 1995, growing about 1.5% per year, highly concentrated: unattractive for entrants.
- Levers: sampling to universities, super premium price (3 to 4 times Coke), athletes and extreme events.
    - Kept producing in Austria to support scarcity and premium image despite shipping costs
- Focused on on-premise channel (clubs, bars) sold by students: cheaper and more credible than a sales force.
    - Bars hold few mixers and the channel is minor for Coke, so Coke could not leverage its strength
- Principle: avoid head-on fights with stronger players (Coke US ad budget 1.6 billion); concentrate force on a weak point ("judo/jiu-jitsu strategy").

#### Monster's Response to Red Bull
- Entered California in 2002: 2X the size at the same $2 price, targeting a lower socioeconomic segment.
- Wide product range needed to win shelf space in supermarkets, the channel Red Bull was not in; third-party US production kept costs competitive.
- Big players like Coke deprioritize energy drinks when fighting Pepsi; acquisitions fail when buyers meddle in marketing and sales (e.g. Grom).
- Sales growing slower than volume shows Monster discounting heavily; the category is price sensitive.

#### Options for Red Bull
- Ideas: keep premium and disciplined, innovate products, new formats, special editions, produce in the US to enter retail.
- Formats drive occasion of use, reduce gaps when product runs out at home, and can improve line efficiency.
- Entering the cola segment was called the most stupid move; lowering price to fight Monster would destroy the company.
- Acquiring Monster was the obvious missed option; Coke bought a stake instead.

#### Takeaways
- Tactics matter, but a strong brand value proposition is needed to last; imitation is a menace, not flattery.
- Price wars are lose-lose; only consumers win.
- Jiu-jitsu niche strategies can be used against you too; pick moves the bigger competitor struggles to follow.

### CV 03-11 — Caso Tesla (Delivering Innovation)
_Data: 2026-11-03 · Argomento del syllabus 14: Delivering Innovation (case: Tesla; Shane ch.7)_

Tesla case: company valuation methods, how expectations drive Tesla's valuation, its simplified business model, crossing the chasm with Model 3, and a shift toward Optimus humanoids.

#### Valuing a Company
- Two common methods: discounted cash flow and multiples (e.g. 8 to 10x EBITDA for a segment)
    - Buyers pay below DCF value (e.g. 80 vs 100) to cover risk, since forecasts are rarely right
- Multiples suit established markets and companies; DCF is hard to apply to new tech startups
- Calculation is the least important part; what matters is the hypotheses driving the numbers
    - Key question: how do you actually win 10 market share points, not how to model its P&L impact

#### Tesla Valuation Driven by Expectations
- In 2020 GM, Ford and Chrysler made far more cars yet were worth less than Tesla's market cap
- Valuation reflects pure expectation: belief that electric is the future and Tesla is a tech/data company
- Back-of-envelope: Tesla market cap per car produced was almost $300,000
- P/E of 444 means centuries to recoup; only sustainable if Tesla becomes the dominant design

#### Market Barriers and Tesla's Business Model
- Automotive barriers: capex, production, R&D, logistics, marketing, dealerships and service network
    - Unlike software, scaling needs full factory capacity
- Tesla's simplifications: direct online sales, no dealerships, no-haggle and free cancellation, web-centered communication
    - Little TV advertising; relied on Elon Musk, word of mouth and stunts
- OTA software updates remove need for dealers to upgrade cars and support vehicle service
- Open-sourcing patents aims to bring others on board so Tesla becomes the dominant design

#### Innovation Strategy and Crossing the Chasm
- First challenge: change the perception of electric cars as slow, low-range golf carts
- Roadster was a marketing success but a commercial disaster; lesson: never retrofit an ICE car, build from scratch
- Crossing the chasm (Moore) needs competitive price and performance: Model S, premium Model X, then Model 3
- Model 3, starting at 35,000, reached the early majority and democratized electric cars

#### Outlook: China, Energy and Optimus
- Chinese EVs cost less than half of comparable European or US cars
- Grid and renewable energy shortfalls limit wider EV adoption
- Tesla dropped a Model 3 production line to build Optimus, using its car data to train the humanoid
- **Dominant design is only identifiable ex post**; hydrogen, electric and AI still compete
- Next: November 10th covers Disney's acquisition of Pixar

[Scelta multipla] Cosa insegna il Roadster? — Opzioni: A) Successo di marketing ma disastro commerciale: mai fare retrofit di un'auto a combustione, costruire da zero | B) Che le auto elettriche non funzionano | C) Che il lusso è l'unico mercato | D) Che bisogna usare concessionari — Soluzione: A. Poi Model S, Model X e Model 3 (35.000$) per attraversare il chasm verso la early majority.
4. [Aperta] Spiega il concetto di 'crossing the chasm' applicato a Tesla e perché l'open-sourcing dei brevetti è coerente con la strategia. — Traccia di soluzione: Il chasm (Moore) è il salto tra early adopter e early majority: serve prezzo e prestazioni competitive. Tesla ha cambiato la percezione (auto elettrica non = golf cart) con Roadster e Model S/X premium, poi Model 3 a 35k ha raggiunto la early majority. Aprire i brevetti porta altri produttori sullo standard Tesla, aumentando la probabilità che diventi il dominant design (e superando barriere come infrastruttura di ricarica).

MISSION
The course develops student’s understanding of how firms use technology and innovation to
position themselves strategically, from the perspective of a Director of R&D, Corporate
Development, and Corporate Venturing. It pays specific attention to the AI transformation which
is impacting all industries in the recent years. For this purpose, it relies heavily on cases and
examples, dealing with firms which have faced the challenges of managing innovation,
technology strategy, and the digital transformation. Students are asked to actively participate in
class discussions to confront the implications of technology and innovation on a firm’s strategy
and to understand actions and reactions by Executives of Business Development when
technology and innovation originate within the firm as well as when they change exogenously
the environment where the firm operates.
