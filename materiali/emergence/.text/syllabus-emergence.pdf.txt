Course objectives
Entrepreneurship is one of the major drivers of economic growth, technological innovation,
and social transformation. This foundational course offers a rigorous, research-based
understanding of entrepreneurship. It addresses a set of core questions: who becomes an
entrepreneur, and why; how entrepreneurs exercise judgment under uncertainty; how ideas
become opportunities; what strategic choices shape the trajectory of ventures; and how
ventures attract customers, raise resources, grow, and exit.
The course deliberately combines theory and practice. Each session reviews the main
theoretical traditions and the most relevant recent empirical evidence on a given aspect of
entrepreneurship and applies them to the discussion of real entrepreneurial cases.
As the first entrepreneurship course of the INTENT master, its goal is not to teach you how to
write a detailed business plan but to give you the conceptual foundations and the evidence-
based posture you will need to interrogate entrepreneurial decisions throughout the program
and your career.
By the end of the course, you will be able to:
• explain who becomes a successful entrepreneur, and why;
• compare frameworks for entrepreneurial action under uncertainty;
• define entrepreneurial opportunities and explain their drivers and the role of the
individual–opportunity nexus;
• explain the role of entrepreneurial hypotheses and their testing;
• compare different types of entrepreneurial strategy;
• describe the key elements of a business model and explain how ventures create and
capture value;
• analyze the dynamics of founding teams, including trade-offs in co-founder choice, role
allocation, and equity;
• identify the main sources and stages of entrepreneurial finance and assess their
implications for control and growth;
• identify the drivers, paths, and challenges of venture growth.

Course format and style
The course leverages a blend of methods aimed at complementing each other and
optimizing the learning experience:
• Lectures review the main theoretical traditions and the most relevant recent empirical
evidence for each topic and use them to test common intuitions about entrepreneurship.
• Case discussions complement the lectures: most sessions discuss a real
entrepreneurial situation that students analyze.
• Guest-speaker talks expose students to the lived experience of accomplished
entrepreneurs and investors, treated as “living cases” that students interrogate with the
frameworks learnt in class.
• A collaborative team assignment lets students put the course’s frameworks into
practice by evaluating a real start-up on the main dimensions covered in the course.
Course materials
All course materials will be distributed through Blackboard. The list of readings is provided at
the beginning of the course and is available to all students, including non-attending students;
copyright-protected readings can be accessed through the University Library’s reserve
service. Slides and any additional materials used in class are made available to all students
through Blackboard.
There is no single required textbook; a useful companion for several sessions is J. Gans, E.
Scott & S. Stern, Entrepreneurship: Choice and Strategy (W. W. Norton, 2024).

Grading
The assessment combines an individual written exam with a collaborative team assignment.
Your final grade is determined as follows:
A. Individual written exam 60%
B. Group project 40%
C. Class participation up to 1 bonus point
Points earned for each component are weighted and summed to obtain a total score for the
course. An overview of each component follows.
A. Individual written exam (60%)
The final exam will include a mix of open- and close-ended questions covering the main
concepts discussed in class, for a total of 30 points. Sample questions will be discussed in
class and posted on Blackboard.
B. Group project (40%)
In self-formed teams, you evaluate a real start-up on the main dimensions covered in the
course (e.g., opportunity, business model, strategy, founding team, financing, and growth).
The assignment tests your ability to apply the theoretical perspectives learnt in the course to
real ventures, to critically evaluate entrepreneurial ideas, business models, and strategic
choices, and to communicate structured analytical reasoning. The output is a written memo.
C. Class participation (up to 1 bonus point)
Class discussion is a central part of the learning experience, and you can earn one bonus
point for consistent and high-quality participation.
Non-attending students
Non-attending students are evaluated solely on an individual written exam, consisting of
open and/or closed questions on the main concepts of the course (slides, cases, and
readings), for a total of 30 points.

Norms of behavior
To have a productive class, we kindly ask you to respect the following:
• Please limit the use of hand-held devices during class. This policy is based on research
documenting their effect on you and on your classmates.
• Communicating with the instructors: please do not send the same request to each of us
separately. Choose the one whose class it relates to, or cc all of us.
• Class representative — please use your class rep to share points of concern, confusion,
and satisfaction; we find it helpful when they gather feedback from the class.
• Re-grading policy: for any re-grading request, we expect a written memo explaining why
the work earned a higher score. If we agree there is cause for re-grading, we will re-
grade the whole work, which may change the grades of other parts as well.
Università Bocconi conceives of education as an ongoing process that stretches across a
person’s entire professional life and asks the whole community to respect the values of
fairness and correctness that inspire its shared mission. We encourage all students to read
the Bocconi University Honor Code, published at http://www.unibocconi.eu/honorcode.
