Entrepreneurial finance lecture on startup funding sources, why startups are hard to finance (information asymmetry, adverse selection, moral hazard), signaling and contracting theories, and bootstrapping, ending with an in-class case exercise.

### Sources of Startup Capital
- Studies show about 40% of initial capital comes from formal credit: bank loans and credit lines
    - Own money and bank debt are likely the two largest sources
- Friends and family funding is about 7 times smaller than bank financing, lower than expected
- Angels and VCs are not the main source, despite most large companies being VC-funded
- Equity crowdfunding was illegal in many countries in the 2014 study, so it scored lower then

### Why Startups Are Hard to Finance
- Startups lack collateral; banks will not lend against lines of code
- High uncertainty makes risk hard to price, so higher interest rates cannot simply offset it
- Information asymmetry: founders know far more about quality and effort than outsiders
- Confident founders may prefer debt to keep equity and control, a strategic choice

### Adverse Selection and Moral Hazard
- Market for lemons (Akerlof): buyer values cars at 10,000 or 5,000, pays 7,500
    - Good sellers exit, leaving only lemons; founders face the same dynamic before funding
- Result is underfunding caused by lack of trust, not lack of money
- Moral hazard after funding: founders may chase vision, lifestyle, or visibility over investor interests

### Signaling and Contracting Theories
- Valid signals must be observable, costly, and costlier for low-quality entrepreneurs
    - Examples: keeping equity, building a prototype, paying customers, credible backers
- Principal agent theory ties incentives to outcomes, e.g. equity and vesting based on thresholds
- Control theory: since perfect contracts are impossible, investors take board seats, votes, and stage investment

### Bootstrapping
- Cluster analysis identifies six bootstrapping types, including owner financing, cash cycle, lean assets, subsidies
    - Shorten cash conversion cycle: less inventory and receivables, more payables
- Pros: total control and discipline; 70% of entrepreneurial firm failures are attributed to lack of cash
- Cons: slower growth, hard to raise 50 to 60 million where speed matters
- Counterexample: Spanx bootstrapped 2000 to 2021, 100% owned, worth over 1 billion
