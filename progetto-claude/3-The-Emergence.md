### The Emergence 5-10 — Finanza imprenditoriale e bootstrapping
_Data: 2026-10-05 · Argomento del syllabus 9: Financing entrepreneurial ventures (case: Jibo)_

Entrepreneurial finance lecture on startup funding sources, why startups are hard to finance (information asymmetry, adverse selection, moral hazard), signaling and contracting theories, and bootstrapping, ending with an in-class case exercise.

#### Sources of Startup Capital
- Studies show about 40% of initial capital comes from formal credit: bank loans and credit lines
    - Own money and bank debt are likely the two largest sources
- Friends and family funding is about 7 times smaller than bank financing, lower than expected
- Angels and VCs are not the main source, despite most large companies being VC-funded
- Equity crowdfunding was illegal in many countries in the 2014 study, so it scored lower then

#### Why Startups Are Hard to Finance
- Startups lack collateral; banks will not lend against lines of code
- High uncertainty makes risk hard to price, so higher interest rates cannot simply offset it
- Information asymmetry: founders know far more about quality and effort than outsiders
- Confident founders may prefer debt to keep equity and control, a strategic choice

#### Adverse Selection and Moral Hazard
- Market for lemons (Akerlof): buyer values cars at 10,000 or 5,000, pays 7,500
    - Good sellers exit, leaving only lemons; founders face the same dynamic before funding
- Result is underfunding caused by lack of trust, not lack of money
- Moral hazard after funding: founders may chase vision, lifestyle, or visibility over investor interests

#### Signaling and Contracting Theories
- Valid signals must be observable, costly, and costlier for low-quality entrepreneurs
    - Examples: keeping equity, building a prototype, paying customers, credible backers
- Principal agent theory ties incentives to outcomes, e.g. equity and vesting based on thresholds
- Control theory: since perfect contracts are impossible, investors take board seats, votes, and stage investment

#### Bootstrapping
- Cluster analysis identifies six bootstrapping types, including owner financing, cash cycle, lean assets, subsidies
    - Shorten cash conversion cycle: less inventory and receivables, more payables
- Pros: total control and discipline; 70% of entrepreneurial firm failures are attributed to lack of cash
- Cons: slower growth, hard to raise 50 to 60 million where speed matters
- Counterexample: Spanx bootstrapped 2000 to 2021, 100% owned, worth over 1 billion

### The Emergence 7-10 — Scaling, imprinting, Penrose (caso Baru)
_Data: 2026-10-07 · Argomento del syllabus 10: Scaling and exit (case: Baroo)_

Lecture on why scaling ventures is hard: founder imprinting, managerial attention limits (Penrose), and the need to formalize during growth, applied to the Baru pet-services case.

#### Defining Scaling
- Scaling means exceptional growth, ideally output growing faster than inputs; restaurant chains scale poorly.
- Scaling is a strategic choice; many founders avoid it to keep control.
    - Only about 3.5% of ~700,000 new US firms grow to large size
    - OECD: fast growth is 20% yearly over 3 years
- Class poll: about 66% chose a consolidation year, about 17% expansion; challenges named included hiring and market demand.

#### Imprinting and Founder Blueprints
- Early founding choices become rules, routines and hierarchies that are rarely revisited.
- Stanford study of 200 tech startups mapped blueprints on attachment, selection, and coordination/control.
    - Founder blueprint predicts the organization better than current CEO's, though evidence is weak
    - At 50 employees: 17.1% managers (commitment) vs about 37% (bureaucracy)
- Study of 167 Silicon Valley startups: founding structure and founder experience strongly predict later executive structure.

#### Imprinting in the Baru Case
- Hourly employee contracts worked early but caused problems once demand grew.
- Luxury building partnerships benefited buildings, not Baru; concierges lacked incentives and sometimes promoted competitors.
- Off-the-shelf booking app fell into disuse; contractual social events and luxury service became burdens in new cities.

#### Allocation: Managerial Attention (Penrose)
- Growth is limited by managers' finite attention, since new hires need training in tacit culture and processes.
- Baru's scarcest resource by summer 2016 was management attention, worsened by founders' maternity leaves and city differences.
- Evidence: fast growth predicts slower later growth only with high tacitness or geographic expansion.
- Hiring five managers at once would not help, as training them consumes remaining leadership time.

#### Adaptation: Formalization During Growth
- US internet ventures 1996 to 2001: formalization and administrative intensity correlated with success (correlational).
- India field experiment with 100 founders: pairing with a formal-management peer improved performance; survival up 10%.
- Baru formalized via outsourced 24/7 hotline and scheduling, losing personalization; workers became overworked.
- Students suggested HR leadership and multi-city hires; culture shifted from pet passion to a job.

### The Emergence 14-10 (parte 1) — Come funzionano i fondi VC
_Data: 2026-10-14 · Argomento del syllabus 11: The industry and the mindset_

Lecture on how venture capital funds work (fundraising, management fees, carry, exits, follow-ons, LPs) plus European and Italian VC statistics.

#### Networking Events
- Value of events depends on the quality of the network
- Slush in Helsinki (November) recommended as a top international VC event

#### How VC Funds Work
- VCs must fundraise from LPs before investing; the speaker is currently raising a new fund and calls it a massive pain
- Cycle: raise, scout deals, negotiate term sheets, manage portfolio, exit; good funds bring 'smart money', crowdfunding is classic 'dumb money'
- Exits are a full sale or selling secondary shares; secondaries typically sold at a 15 to 20 percent discount
    - Speaker's funds had three exits; two secondaries at 4.5x to 5.5x, forced by lack of follow-on capital
- Seed funds deploy capital in 2 to 3 years; venture or growth funds take 6 to 7 years

#### Fund Economics: 2 and 20
- Management fees of about 2% of AUM per year cover operations; carry is 20% of profits after returning LP capital plus hurdle rate
- Partners and principals typically put in 1 to 2% of the fund themselves, sometimes via loans, to access carry
- Classic seed split: 55 to 60 percent new investments, 40 to 45 percent follow-ons into winners
    - Follow-ons are where funds make most of their money, hence the push for hockey-stick growth
- Example fund (Partech) grew from about 40 to 500 million AUM with 12 to 13 people to about 3.5 billion with 60 people

#### LPs, Deal Flow and Founder Advice
- LP types: institutional (e.g. Cassa Depositi e Prestiti), pension funds, family offices, corporates seeking tech and acquisition targets
- Italian pension funds rarely invest in VC due to tax framework and weak exit track record
- Funds say no 98 to 99% of the time: about 3,000 to 4,000 deals seen, 30 to 40 investments per year
- Judge VCs by how quickly and clearly they say no; avoid giving away too much equity out of desperation

#### European and Italian VC Landscape
- Europe 2025: 69 billion, 46% below 2021 and 3.4 times smaller than the US; 61% linked to AI/ML
- Italy 2025: 1.7 billion over 434 rounds, 80% seed and pre-seed, versus UK 10 to 11 billion, France about 8 billion, Spain 3.5 billion
- Italy 2026: 1.3 billion by end of September, skewed by one 234 million round; expected 1.8 to 1.9 billion
- International investors in 65% of amount but only 30% of rounds; Italy needs stronger deal flow to attract them

### The Emergence 14-10 (parte 2) — Comfort zone e mindset (Robert Capa)
_Data: 2026-10-14 · Argomento del syllabus 11: The industry and the mindset_

Lecture on working inside versus outside the comfort zone, using war photographer Robert Capa as a parallel for founders: get close, take reasonable risks, keep expanding your comfort zone.

#### Comfort Zone Basics
- Comfort zone means low effort, low stress and low risk, but rarely exciting results or career growth
- Staying comfortable is fine in some life phases, leaving more time for family, health and hobbies
- Leaving it brings new skills, novelty and earning potential, but also greater risk of loss

#### Robert Capa as Case Study
- Born Endre Friedmann in Budapest, 1913; fled to Paris and reinvented himself as a war photographer
- D-Day Omaha Beach photo: he landed ahead of soldiers, back to enemy fire, shooting a manual Leica
- Blurry shots became the book 'Slightly Out of Focus'; the photo is the tip of the iceberg of preparation
- Died stepping on a landmine minutes after his last photo in Indochina; risk grows with what you put in

#### Lessons for Founders
- 'If your pictures aren't good enough, you're not close enough': stay close to your market
- Comfort zone should grow daily; aim to become comfortable with being uncomfortable
- Example: founders handling a lawsuit from driving schools without crumbling
- Half measures rarely build great companies; co-founders should not have side gigs

#### Q&A and Personal Example
- Pushing past discomfort (e.g. job applications that feel out of reach) always pays off
- Speaker got a fund principal role by approaching the managing partner right after an LP meeting
- Most people projecting genius are good at self-presentation; research roles to judge real fit
- Balance humility and confidence; avoid crossing into cockiness

### The Emergence 04-11 (parte 1) — Costi del fundraising e product-market fit
_Data: 2026-11-04 · Argomento del syllabus 12: Building before you raise_

Why fundraising should not be a founder's first priority: updated Italian/European VC figures, costs of raising (dilution, lost autonomy), VC-backed vs bootstrapped, and reaching product-market fit first.

#### VC Market Update
- Italy near 1.7 billion invested by end of October 2026, possibly a first 2 billion euro year
- Amount invested rising but number of rounds falling as seed-stage companies mature into Series A
    - Risk: fewer seed rounds may not feed the funnel of future companies
- Europe's largest 2026 rounds (e.g. Mistral AI, 3 billion euro) dwarfed by US 444 billion euro vs Europe 68, Asia 102
- Open question: should Europe focus on strengths like robotics, life sciences, deep tech rather than core AI and software?

#### The Real Cost of Fundraising
- Raising money is not a measure of success; Mailchimp, GitHub, Basecamp, Notion grew bootstrapped for years
- Raising means dilution, and high valuations raise down-round risk at the next round
- Formal autonomy loss: voting vs equity, veto rights, drag/tag-along, protective provisions on debt, board seats from Series A
    - Tag-along protects minority investors; drag-along lets majority force a full sale
    - Speaker's fund: A shares hold all votes; C shares may own 60% with no votes
- Informal loss: scaling pressure, constant reporting, obligation to spend raised money, time-consuming investor relations
    - Prefer fewer, larger investors; pre-brief board members so meetings just formalize decisions

#### VC-Backed vs Bootstrapped
- VC-backed firms grow at all costs, burn cash, chase next rounds and plan exits; bootstrapped aim for profit from day one and full control
    - Example: speaker's fund exited via secondary sale at 30% discount, about 4.5x in three years
- VCs increasingly also want sustainable business models; good companies succeed either way
- Banks look for bootstrapped qualities; some VCs now offer venture debt alongside equity
- Fundraising is slow (about 100 emails per investment), distracts from running the business, and feedback must be filtered systematically

#### Before Fundraising: Team and Users
- Startups begin confused about product, customers and revenue (Dave McClure quote)
- Team: transparent talks on roles and commitment, fair cap table, vesting (1-year cliff, 25%, then to 100% over 4 years), obsession with execution, focus
- Users: build something few users love (Sam Altman); use open, unbiased interview questions and iterate quickly with an MVP
- Loving users become free ambassadors and reveal willingness to pay

#### Product-Market Fit and Metrics
- Product-market fit: about 40% of users would be very disappointed if the product disappeared (Sean Ellis test, cited with Lean Startup)
- Key metrics: NPS (0-6 detractors, 7-8 neutral/passives, 9-10 promoters), organic traffic, retention/churn, paying users, LTV/CAC
    - Churned users give the most valuable feedback; indifferent churn is a bad sign
- Right metrics change by stage, and product-market fit is temporary as markets, laws and competitors shift
- Building a product differs from building a company; founders must shift from product manager to business manager

### The Emergence 04-11 (parte 2) — Preparare il fundraising, casi Notion/Patreon
_Data: 2026-11-04 · Argomento del syllabus 12: Building before you raise_

Fundraising preparation (investor updates, pitch deck, one-pager, data room, pitching), followed by a breakout where students presented early-stage histories of Notion, Patreon and others.

#### Preparing to Fundraise
- Send warm contacts a short, regular update (monthly to quarterly) with real milestones: features, clients, key hires.
    - Signals momentum and organization, helps you land one of the ~10 investor meetings
- Target funds by sector and stage; sending a software deck to a life science fund wastes everyone's time.
- Pitch deck must be very concise and clear; investors see thousands each year, so deep homework is needed behind it.
- Prepare a one-pager: team, market, need, product and solution, traction, cap table.

#### Data Room, Momentum and Pitching
- A data room is a well-structured folder: contracts, patents, financials, pipeline, office leases. Have it ready before fundraising.
- Delaying key documents kills investor momentum; aim to share the data room the same or next day when asked.
- Practice the pitch in a mirror, on video, and with honest friends; be confident, not cocky.

#### Course Assessment (from side discussion)
- Group assignment is a PDF memo with up to 5 exhibits, no slides required.
- **Group work 40%, exam 60%; participation gives up to 1.5 bonus points.**

#### Breakout: Company Case Studies
- Notion: friends as team and users, burned 2 million, rebuilt in Japan, then raised 10 million (2019), 50 million (2020), 275 (2021).
    - Lesson: friends give biased feedback and are not the target users
- Patreon (Jack Conte, Sam Yam): grew via YouTube, seed of about 2.5 million after five months; first 40 creators emailed rejected them.
- Psychology service for expats via Facebook groups: bootstrapped 2019, 150,000 euro in 2020, 17 million in 2023.
- A coffee chain tested one shop as a lab for seven months, profitable from early shops, later raised from General Atlantic.

#### Closing Takeaways
- Fundraising should not be the first priority; solve a problem, build team, know users and product first.
- Raising money means giving away equity, autonomy and time.
- Product market fit is measurable: NPS, retention, churn; track the one number that matters now.
- Use warm intros and build investor relationships even when not raising.

### The Emergence 02-11 (guest, parte 1) — Strategia di scale-up
_Data: 2026-11-02 · Argomento del syllabus 13: Guest lecture Colao (I): scale-up strategy_

Guest lecture by Vittorio on scaling up growth-stage companies, using a hypothetical 100 million revenue internal-communications SaaS case to debate investment timing, Microsoft and AI risk, organization, and sales.

#### Framing: Growth Investing and the Case
- Speaker drew on consulting, a scale-up that became Vodafone, government, and now growth private equity (not venture).
    - Agenda: scale-up strategy, organization and team, expansion (geography, product, adjacencies), financing
- Growth stage: significant revenue (50 to 300-400 million), little debt, a defensible moat, judged by the rule of 40.
    - Rule of 40 = revenue growth % + EBITDA margin %; rule of 20-25 needs a fix
- Case: internal-comms SaaS, 10 years old, 2 founders, 700 people, 100 million revenue, break-even, 10-15% growth (rule of 15).
    - 50% Europe, 40% USA; integrated web, email and app; less than a euro per seat per month

#### Timing, Adoption and Moat
- Key questions: when it will deliver, cost, financing, adoption speed, and whether there is a real moat.
    - Free-to-premium works for some products, others never monetize if free
- Growth deals have models and data, but tech evolution and adoption remain uncertain; expert calls often polarized.
- Examples: level 4 driverless cars, robotaxis likely a mixed human/robot fleet given peak-hour capital cost.
- Mis-estimated adoption: Spotify underestimated, Duolingo up then down, Revolut, Peloton lost 95%, Match, Zoom lacked a moat.
    - Competitive disruption: Novo Nordisk vs Eli Lilly, Tesla vs BYD, China in autonomy

#### Case Debate: Category Leader vs Microsoft Risk
- Bull view: integrated product defines the category; focused founders out-execute a distracted Microsoft.
- Bear view: Microsoft could easily add the feature or give it free, as happened to Zoom.
- Only about 10-15% of students said they would invest at this stage.

#### Organization and Culture in Scale-ups
- Startup phase needs adventurers and risk takers; growth phase needs curious planners with business cases.
    - Expect and accept healthy conflict between the two cultures; keep the DNA
- Wireless Logic left 15 acquired CEOs independent, causing product complexity; now centralizing the product plan.
- At Vodafone: 70-30 split of common plan vs local innovation; M-Pesa in Kenya.
    - A separate low-cost brand unit in Milan failed when corporate functions blocked it
- Sustained scale-up culture is rare; some large firms (Microsoft, Google, JP Morgan) do it reasonably.

#### Case Debate: AI, Sales and the Buyer
- AI split views: better targeted content vs easy replication by customers or cheap competitors.
    - Survivors: systems of record, and products where building in-house is uneconomic for mid-size firms
- At under a euro per seat, a 1,000-person firm pays about 8,000 to 12,000 euros per year, so building in-house rarely pays.
- "Data must have legs": analytics matter only with a strong salesforce acting on them.
- Decision maker unclear: HR favors engagement, IT resists complexity, internal comms may defer to Microsoft.

### The Emergence 02-11 (guest, parte 2) — Percorsi di crescita, funding, exit
_Data: 2026-11-02 · Argomento del syllabus 13: Guest lecture Colao (I): scale-up strategy_

Guest lecture (part 2) on scale-up growth paths (geography, product, adjacencies), funding choices and exits, using a live SaaS case later revealed as the speaker's own company, plus leadership traits.

#### Three Growth Paths and Their Traps
- Growth options: new geographies, product extensions, or adjacent businesses (e.g. restaurant to hotel)
- Geography checks: business model, local competitors, hiring/culture, channels, regulation; Europe is often a trap
- Product extension needs research budget and engineering cost; selling to a new buyer can double commercial cost
- Adjacencies can destroy value, but work when channels or technology platform are shared

#### Portfolio Examples
- French health-booking firm (likely Doctolib) struggled in Italy, succeeded in Germany
    - Italian doctors paid fixed per patient, not per appointment; small practices; private hospitals refused to share slots
- Phone refurbisher (Back Market) moving into e-bikes added repair and logistics complexity, limited success
- Wireless Logic moved from IoT to travel eSIMs: same core competence and channel, very successful

#### Class Debate on the SaaS Case
- Ideas: improve product and sales funnel; expand to Southeast Asia, Latin America, Africa at low cost
- Software travels at near zero cost, but sales channels, agents and commissions are not free
- Adding HR or task management risks competing with SAP and Microsoft and doing many things poorly
- Internal comms consulting seen as promising since process is not proprietary; custom builds don't scale (compared to Celonis)

#### Funding Rounds and Exits
- Highest valuation is fine only for a last round; a later down round hurts stock-option hiring
- Few large vs many small investors: control and network vs governance complexity; no single right answer
- Cost of capital: equity 15-20%, debt 8-9%, hybrid 12-13%; equity buys expertise and dry powder
- Exits: IPO, trade sale, roll-up, leverage, dividends; founder secondary sales signal confidence levels

#### Case Reveal and Leadership Traits
- Case company: Staffbase (CEO Martin Böhringer); the speaker is chairman
    - Cut staff via AI, consolidator in sector, IPO paused, active in Europe, US, Asia-Pacific
- Student exit votes: M&A consolidation, sell to Microsoft, fix business structure and sales
- Successful leaders: product perfectionists, non-dogmatic on strategy, people-focused, medium-term vision
- Keep 'listening antennas': read widely and spend lots of time with team and partners

1. [Scelta multipla] Cosa rappresenta la frase di Robert Capa 'If your pictures aren't good enough, you're not close enough' per un founder? — Opzioni: A) Investire in tecnologia migliore | B) Stare vicino al proprio mercato e ai clienti | C) Assumere fotografi per il marketing | D) Evitare i rischi — Soluzione: B. Lezione: avvicinarsi, prendere rischi ragionevoli ed espandere continuamente la comfort zone.
2. [Aperta] Quali lezioni per i founder si traggono dalla parte 'mindset' della lezione? Citane almeno tre. — Traccia di soluzione: La comfort zone deve crescere ogni giorno: diventare 'comfortable with being uncomfortable'; le mezze misure raramente costruiscono grandi aziende (co-founder senza side gig); il rischio cresce con ciò che metti in gioco; la foto è la punta dell'iceberg della preparazione; bilanciare umiltà e sicurezza senza diventare arroganti; spingersi oltre il disagio (es. candidature che sembrano fuori portata) paga.

### Domande — The Emergence 04-11 (parte 1) — Costi del fundraising e product-market fit
1. [Scelta multipla] Quale soglia indica il product-market fit secondo il test citato? — Opzioni: A) Il 10% degli utenti paga | B) Circa il 40% degli utenti sarebbe 'molto deluso' se il prodotto sparisse | C) NPS sopra 0 | D) 1.000 utenti attivi — Soluzione: B. Altre metriche: NPS, traffico organico, retention/churn, utenti paganti, LTV/CAC. Il PMF è temporaneo.
2. [Scelta multipla] Nel Net Promoter Score, come si classificano i voti? — Opzioni: A) 0-6 detractor, 7-8 passivi/neutri, 9-10 promoter | B) 0-4 detractor, 5-7 neutri, 8-10 promoter | C) 0-5 detractor, 6-10 promoter | D) Solo 10 è promoter — Soluzione: A. NPS = % promoter − % detractor.
3. [Scelta multipla] Quale schema di vesting è stato indicato come standard? — Opzioni: A) Tutto subito | B) Cliff di 1 anno (25%), poi fino al 100% in 4 anni | C) Cliff di 6 mesi, 2 anni totali | D) Nessun vesting tra co-founder — Soluzione: B. Parte della preparazione del team prima di raccogliere: ruoli e commitment chiari, cap table equa, vesting.
4. [Scelta multipla] Quali sono perdite di autonomia FORMALI legate al fundraising? — Opzioni: A) Diritti di veto e protective provisions | B) Drag-along/tag-along | C) Board seat dalla Series A | D) Pressione costante al reporting — Soluzione: A, B, C. La pressione al reporting, l'obbligo di spendere e il tempo per investor relations sono perdite informali.
5. [Aperta] Spiega la differenza tra drag-along e tag-along e chi protegge ciascuna clausola. — Traccia di soluzione: Tag-along: protegge i soci di minoranza, che possono unirsi alla vendita alle stesse condizioni se la maggioranza vende. Drag-along: permette alla maggioranza di obbligare tutti i soci a vendere (exit totale).

### Domande — The Emergence 04-11 (parte 2) — Preparare il fundraising, casi Notion/Patreon
1. [Scelta multipla] Cosa NON dovrebbe mancare in un one-pager per investitori? — Opzioni: A) Team, mercato, bisogno, prodotto/soluzione, traction, cap table | B) Il codice sorgente | C) Il business plan dettagliato a 10 anni | D) I CV completi di tutti i dipendenti — Soluzione: A. Il pitch deck deve essere molto conciso: gli investitori ne vedono migliaia all'anno.
2. [Scelta multipla] Perché la data room va preparata PRIMA del fundraising? — Opzioni: A) Perché è obbligatoria per legge | B) Perché ritardare i documenti chiave uccide il momentum dell'investitore: va condivisa lo stesso giorno o il successivo | C) Perché sostituisce il pitch | D) Perché la chiedono solo i fondi pensione — Soluzione: B. Contiene contratti, brevetti, financials, pipeline, contratti d'affitto, ben strutturati.
3. [Aperta] Quale lezione insegna il caso Notion sul rapporto tra utenti, prodotto e fundraising? — Traccia di soluzione: Notion partì con amici come team e utenti, bruciò ~2M, ricostruì il prodotto in Giappone, poi raccolse 10M (2019), 50M (2020), 275M (2021). Lezione: gli amici danno feedback distorti e non sono il target; prima si risolve un problema per utenti veri e si costruisce il prodotto, poi si raccoglie.
4. [Aperta] Come è composto il voto di questo corso e cosa vale la partecipazione? — Traccia di soluzione: 60% esame scritto individuale, 40% group project (memo PDF con max 5 exhibit); partecipazione fino a +1 punto bonus secondo il syllabus (in aula si è parlato di 1,5).

### Domande — The Emergence 02-11 (guest, parte 1) — Strategia di scale-up
1. [Scelta multipla] Come si calcola la Rule of 40 citata da Colao per le aziende growth stage? — Opzioni: A) Crescita ricavi % + EBITDA margin % | B) Crescita ricavi % × margine lordo | C) EBITDA / ricavi > 40% | D) Numero di dipendenti / ricavi — Soluzione: A. Il caso (SaaS da 100M, break-even, crescita 10-15%) ha una 'rule of 15': va sistemato.
2. [Scelta multipla] Quale rischio principale del caso SaaS di internal communication è stato discusso (bear view)? — Opzioni: A) Che Microsoft aggiunga la funzione o la offra gratis, come successo a Zoom | B) Che i ricavi siano troppo alti | C) Che i founder vendano troppo presto | D) Che il prodotto non sia integrato — Soluzione: A. Bull view: leader di categoria con founder focalizzati che superano in esecuzione una Microsoft distratta.
3. [Aperta] Perché startup e scale-up richiedono culture diverse e come andrebbe gestito il conflitto? Usa almeno un esempio della lezione. — Traccia di soluzione: Startup: avventurieri e risk taker; growth: pianificatori curiosi con business case. Il conflitto sano va accettato mantenendo il DNA. Esempi: Wireless Logic lasciò indipendenti 15 CEO acquisiti → complessità di prodotto, ora centralizza; Vodafone split 70-30 piano comune vs innovazione locale (M-Pesa in Kenya); unità low-cost separata a Milano fallita perché bloccata dalle funzioni corporate.

### Domande — The Emergence 02-11 (guest, parte 2) — Percorsi di crescita, funding, exit
1. [Scelta multipla] Quali sono i tre percorsi di crescita di una scale-up? — Opzioni: A) Nuove geografie, estensioni di prodotto, business adiacenti | B) IPO, M&A, debito | C) Prezzo, prodotto, promozione | D) Seed, Series A, Series B — Soluzione: A. Le adiacenze funzionano quando canali o piattaforma tecnologica sono condivisi (Wireless Logic: da IoT a eSIM per viaggi).
2. [Scelta multipla] Perché Doctolib ha faticato in Italia ma ha avuto successo in Germania? — Opzioni: A) Problemi di traduzione | B) Medici italiani pagati a quota fissa per paziente e non per visita, studi piccoli, ospedali privati che non condividevano slot | C) Concorrenza di Google | D) Regolamentazione GDPR solo italiana — Soluzione: B. Esempio dei controlli da fare sulla geografia: business model, competitor locali, cultura, canali, regolamentazione.
3. [Scelta multipla] Secondo il relatore, quale costo del capitale è tipicamente più alto? — Opzioni: A) Debito (8-9%) | B) Ibrido (12-13%) | C) Equity (15-20%) | D) Sono uguali — Soluzione: C. L'equity costa di più ma porta competenze e 'dry powder'.
4. [Aperta] Perché la valutazione più alta possibile non è sempre la scelta migliore in un round? — Traccia di soluzione: Va bene solo se è l'ultimo round: se la successiva valutazione è più bassa (down round) si danneggiano le stock option e quindi la capacità di assumere e trattenere talenti, oltre al segnale negativo al mercato.

Course objectives
Entrepreneurship is one of the major drivers of economic growth, technological innovation,
and social transformation. This foundational course offers a rigorous, research-based
understanding of entrepreneurship. It addresses a set of core questions: who becomes an
entrepreneur, and why; how entrepreneurs exercise judgment under uncertainty; how ideas
become opportunities; what strategic choices shape the trajectory of ventures; and how
ventures attract customers, raise resources, grow, and exit.
The course deliberately combines theory and practice. Each session reviews the main
theoretical traditions and the most relevant recent empirical evidence on a given aspect of
entrepreneurship and applies them to the discussion of real entrepreneurial cases.
As the first entrepreneurship course of the INTENT master, its goal is not to teach you how to
write a detailed business plan but to give you the conceptual foundations and the evidence-
based posture you will need to interrogate entrepreneurial decisions throughout the program
and your career.
By the end of the course, you will be able to:
• explain who becomes a successful entrepreneur, and why;
• compare frameworks for entrepreneurial action under uncertainty;
• define entrepreneurial opportunities and explain their drivers and the role of the
individual–opportunity nexus;
• explain the role of entrepreneurial hypotheses and their testing;
• compare different types of entrepreneurial strategy;
• describe the key elements of a business model and explain how ventures create and
capture value;
• analyze the dynamics of founding teams, including trade-offs in co-founder choice, role
allocation, and equity;
• identify the main sources and stages of entrepreneurial finance and assess their
implications for control and growth;
• identify the drivers, paths, and challenges of venture growth.

Course format and style
The course leverages a blend of methods aimed at complementing each other and
optimizing the learning experience:
• Lectures review the main theoretical traditions and the most relevant recent empirical
evidence for each topic and use them to test common intuitions about entrepreneurship.
• Case discussions complement the lectures: most sessions discuss a real
entrepreneurial situation that students analyze.
• Guest-speaker talks expose students to the lived experience of accomplished
entrepreneurs and investors, treated as “living cases” that students interrogate with the
frameworks learnt in class.
• A collaborative team assignment lets students put the course’s frameworks into
practice by evaluating a real start-up on the main dimensions covered in the course.

Grading
The assessment combines an individual written exam with a collaborative team assignment.
Your final grade is determined as follows:
A. Individual written exam 60%
B. Group project 40%
C. Class participation up to 1 bonus point
Points earned for each component are weighted and summed to obtain a total score for the
course. An overview of each component follows.
A. Individual written exam (60%)
The final exam will include a mix of open- and close-ended questions covering the main
concepts discussed in class, for a total of 30 points. Sample questions will be discussed in
class and posted on Blackboard.
B. Group project (40%)
In self-formed teams, you evaluate a real start-up on the main dimensions covered in the
course (e.g., opportunity, business model, strategy, founding team, financing, and growth).
The assignment tests your ability to apply the theoretical perspectives learnt in the course to
real ventures, to critically evaluate entrepreneurial ideas, business models, and strategic
choices, and to communicate structured analytical reasoning. The output is a written memo.
C. Class participation (up to 1 bonus point)
Class discussion is a central part of the learning experience, and you can earn one bonus
point for consistent and high-quality participation.
Non-attending students
Non-attending students are evaluated solely on an individual written exam, consisting of
open and/or closed questions on the main concepts of the course (slides, cases, and
readings), for a total of 30 points.
